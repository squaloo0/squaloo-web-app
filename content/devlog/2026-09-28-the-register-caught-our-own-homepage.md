We keep a file called the claims register. Every number we say in public has a row in it: the number, where it came from, what date it was true, and what boundary it holds within. If a claim cannot get a row, it does not get said.

Last week the register turned on its own homepage.

## The number

Every deck, every application, every version of our front page carried the same figure for what an hour of unplanned downtime costs heavy industry: **$125,000 to $5 million per hour.** It is the number the whole business case is sized against. It had been in our materials for a month.

The register had it flagged — not as wrong, but as **unsourced**. Nobody could say where it came from. That is its own status in our vocabulary, and it exists because a claim can be well-formed, correctly bounded, consistently repeated, and still rest on nothing.

So we went looking.

## What the trail actually showed

The number appeared in our market research, citing our strategy document, citing our own earlier research. Internal documents citing each other in a loop — which is exactly why nobody had ever found the end of it. Everyone had seen it somewhere credible, and "somewhere credible" was us.

The loop terminated in a real table, with real sources. Here it is:

| sector | hourly cost | source |
|---|---|---|
| Food & beverage | $4,000 – $30,000 | ABB Value of Reliability Survey |
| **General manufacturing (median)** | **~$125,000** | ABB Value of Reliability Survey |
| Pharmaceuticals | $100,000 – $500,000 | Siemens, *The True Cost of Downtime 2024* |
| Oil & gas | $200,000 – $500,000+ | Siemens, same |
| **Automotive** | **up to $2.3 million** | Siemens, same |
| Heavy industry (maximum) | up to $59 million | Siemens, same |

Read the table against our claim.

**The floor is real.** $125,000 is right there, correctly attributed, correctly characterised as a median.

**The ceiling is not in the table at all.** There is no $5 million row. There is no aerospace row. Our figure had been attributed to "advanced aerospace fabrication" — a sector the source never covers — and it sat one rung above a real $2.3 million automotive number, which is the tell. Someone had reasoned *aerospace is more expensive than automotive*, rounded up, and written it down. Everyone after that was quoting a colleague.

That is not an uncited number. It is an **invented** one, and it had our name on it.

## Why this is the interesting failure and not the embarrassing one

The embarrassing version of this story is "a startup exaggerated." That is not what happened, and pretending it was would miss the useful part.

Nobody decided to inflate anything. The number entered as background, got repeated by people who assumed someone upstream had checked, and hardened into fact through nothing but repetition. Every individual step was reasonable. The outcome was a fabricated figure on a public website.

We now have a name for this in the register: **unsourced inheritance.** It joins two failure modes we had already written down — overclaiming, and the less obvious one, *stale underclaiming*, where you keep apologising for a limitation you have since fixed.

The register also records something we would rather not advertise: it caught this in our materials **and in its own pages**. The same file that polices our claims had a row citing a 100% result against a single observation. Same shape, same week, committed by the person maintaining the register.

## What changed

The homepage now reads **$125K–$2.3M per hour**, with ABB and Siemens named on the page itself. The ceiling is retired everywhere. The correction is written into the register in place rather than edited out, because a correction that erases its own history teaches nobody.

And one rule became syntax rather than etiquette: **a number we cannot recompute from our committed evidence may not be quoted.** Not "should be checked" — may not be quoted.

We wrote that rule down and it caught us the same afternoon. The corrected figure for a different claim was computed, written as 19 of 19, and was **stale before it reached the file** — an overnight run had landed and made it 20. That one is genuinely funny, and it is why every number on this site now carries the date it was true, plus the method to recompute it yourself.

## The part that matters

A demo-day representative and a professor were scheduled to look at our site four days later. They would not have caught this. Nobody catches this from the outside; the number looks exactly like every other industry statistic in every other pitch.

The only thing that catches it is a process that assumes we are wrong and goes looking, on a schedule, including in the places we are proudest of.

That process is also the product. We sell a system that makes AI show where its answers came from, because confident and correct are different things. It would be difficult to sell that honestly while running a company that had never once asked itself the same question.
